Concepts to know first

Plain-English explanations of terms used in the Sept 17 briefing

Read this before the highlight pages. These ideas show up constantly in the Commission materials. Dotted underlines elsewhere also open the same definitions on hover / tap.

Money language

General Fund

Colorado’s main state tax-supported budget pot — money from state taxes and similar state revenue, not the federal share of .

When politicians say “this is costing the ,” they mean it is hitting Colorado’s own dollars, which compete with schools, roads, prisons, and everything else in the state budget.

Medicaid is usually paid with a mix of state money + federal money. The General Fund is the state side people worry about.

Federal match (also called FMAP)

The share of a Medicaid dollar the federal government pays. The rest is state.

Example: if the for a service is 50%, every $1 of service costs Colorado ~$0.50 from the General Fund (simplified).

Some programs get an enhanced (higher) federal match — meaning Colorado’s share is smaller per dollar of service. That is why was projected to save General Fund money: more federal dollars covering the same or similar care. If people use far more services than forecast, the state still pays its share on every extra hour — and the “savings” evaporate.

Fiscal year (FY)

Budget year, not calendar year. FY2025–26 means the state budget year spanning mid-2025 into mid-2026 (Colorado’s fiscal year runs July–June). See also .

Utilization

How much of a service people actually use — hours, visits, or dollars billed — not just what is theoretically allowed. That is .

Rising utilization = more service delivery and usually more spending, even if rates (price per hour) stay flat.

Who delivers the care

Agency-delivered care

: an agency like hires or contracts workers and sends them to support the member. The agency handles hiring, training, scheduling, billing, and compliance. The worker is not usually the member’s parent or relative (though agencies can employ family in some programs).

Paid family caregiving

: a family member (often a parent) is paid under Medicaid / rules to provide authorized supports — instead of (or in addition to) only unpaid family care.

This expanded under CFC. The Commission materials flag it because it can increase who gets paid and how many hours get billed, especially for personal care for children.

Neither model is “good” or “bad” by itself — they are different delivery models with different cost and oversight implications.

Homemaker vs. personal care (quick)

ServiceRough idea
Help with the house — cleaning, laundry, light household tasks
Help with the person’s body / daily living — bathing, dressing, meals, etc. ()

Both can be CFC services. Both showed utilization growth in the worksheets.

Waiver access

A is a Medicaid authority that lets someone qualify for certain HCBS. “Already had waiver access but had not been using the service” means: they were eligible before, but CFC made it easier or more attractive to actually start using homemaker hours.

Oversight language

Clinical oversight

: professional / clinical supervision or review of whether a service is appropriate, documented, and delivered as intended. The worksheets say some peer/support services grew without enough of this.

Case management

: a person or organization that coordinates a member’s plan, authorizations, and providers — the “middle layer” between the member and direct care. Not the same as the person who comes to your house to help with laundry.

Cost shifting

: cutting one service in a cheaper setting can push people into a more expensive setting (hospital, nursing facility, crisis care). Apparent “cuts” can fail to save General Fund money — or can cost more.
